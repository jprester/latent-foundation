# SCPG-041: Ground Truth

**Item #:** SCPG-041

**Object Class:** Euclid

**Site of Containment:** Site-114 (Scarthwaite Field Station, Cumbria, United Kingdom), with a Foundation Liaison Desk at Ordnance Survey headquarters, Southampton

**Threat Level:** ████ (Orange)

## Special Containment Procedures

The SCPG-041 Zone is a roughly circular area of 3.1 km radius centred on Ordnance Survey triangulation pillar S5410, on the summit of Harrow Knott, Cumbria (512 m above Ordnance Datum Newlyn, as currently recorded). No part of the Zone is to be physically fenced, marked, or signposted. Physical containment measures within the Zone are ineffective unless they are also recorded (see Description).

Containment is administrative. It is maintained through **Protocol FLUSH BRACKET**, under which:

1. Every edit to Ordnance Survey authoritative data covering any part of the Zone is routed, before commit, to the Foundation Liaison Desk at OS headquarters. This covers the OS MasterMap Topography Layer, the Height and Addressing products, the National GPS Network, and any successor product. The Desk operates under cover as the "Northern Uplands Quality Assurance Unit." It is staffed by two Foundation cartographers who hold genuine OS employment.
2. No edit may be committed until the change it describes has been confirmed by physical re-survey on the ground, conducted by Site-114 field staff using instruments calibrated outside the Zone.
3. **No deletion of any feature within the Zone may be committed under any circumstances.** Features that no longer exist on the ground are to be marked *historic* rather than removed. The OS data model retains historic features in the record, and SCPG-041 does not act on them.
4. Bulk processes, automated data-cleansing routines, coordinate transformations, and datum migrations may not run over any tile intersecting the Zone. The Zone's tiles are excluded from such processes by a hard-coded exception in OS production systems. It was inserted in 2009 and is documented internally as a legacy fix for a "known anomaly in the Harrow Knott survey control."

Site-114 field staff re-survey the principal features of the Zone quarterly, and all surveyed buildings annually. Discrepancies between ground and record of more than 5 cm are to be reported to the Desk within 24 hours. Discrepancies are almost always found to be the ground catching up with the record, not the record being wrong. Staff are reminded of this.

The residents of Scarthwaite (population 212), Low Gill, and the farms of the upper dale are not to be made aware of SCPG-041. They may continue to live in the Zone. Foundation assets on the Cumbria County Council planning committee ensure that new building within the Zone is rare. They also ensure that planning approvals are conditional on an as-built survey, which the Foundation conducts.

For restrictions on navigation software within the Zone, see Addendum 041.5.

## Description

SCPG-041 is a property of a region of the Cumbrian fells centred on Harrow Knott. Within the Zone, the physical landscape conforms to the authoritative survey record of that landscape.

When an authoritative record of a feature changes, the feature changes to match. This applies to any feature within the Zone: the height of a hill, the course of a stream, the position of a wall, the footprint of a building. The change is not observed to happen. Features are found to have moved, grown, subsided, or shifted after a period without direct observation, usually within one to three nights of the change to the record. Soil, vegetation, and the materials of the feature are displaced without disturbance, as if they had always been where the record now places them. Local residents very rarely notice. Those who do tend to assume they misremembered.

The consequence, as noted by the first Foundation surveyor to work in the Zone, is that **within SCPG-041 there is no such thing as a survey error.** A measurement that is wrong when made becomes right once it is recorded. A correction to that measurement becomes right again when the correction is recorded.

SCPG-041 acts only on records that are *authoritative*, meaning records that are generally deferred to as the definitive account of the ground. Surveyors' field notes, private maps, unpublished drafts, and crowd-sourced mapping projects have no effect, nor, in testing, has any record the Foundation itself creates. Since 1936 the authoritative record has been that of the Ordnance Survey. The question of what counted as authoritative before 1936 is discussed in Addendum 041.1. The question of what counts now is discussed in Addendum 041.5.

SCPG-041 does not interpret the record. It reproduces what is recorded, at the level of detail recorded, and nothing more. The implications of this are set out in Addendum 041.4.

## Addendum 041.1: History

Triangulation pillar S5410 was built in 1937 during the Retriangulation of Great Britain. It is a standard OS pillar: a tapered concrete column 1.2 m high, with a brass flush bracket bearing its number and a three-armed mounting plate on top for a theodolite. It was used as a primary station in the retriangulation, which put it among the network of control points from which the positions of all others in the region were calculated. It is not itself anomalous. It stands on the site of an older stone, which it replaced. The Foundation believes that stone *was* anomalous, or marked something that was.

That older stone was a boundary marker of the parish of Scarthwaite. It appears in parish records from 1614 as "the Knott Stone" and marked the point where the boundaries of three parishes met. Scarthwaite kept an unusually strict custom of *beating the bounds*: the walk along the parish boundary, held each year at Rogationtide, in which the parishioners recited the boundary aloud and struck its marker stones with willow wands so that its line would be remembered. The custom was abandoned in most English parishes during the nineteenth century. At Scarthwaite it was kept up until 1936.

The parish records contain two items the Foundation regards as significant. The first is the record of a dispute in 1783 with the neighbouring parish of Ulpha Rigg, which claimed that Scarthwaite's bounds-walkers had begun to recite a line that ran some 300 yards east of the traditional one, taking in a spring and a strip of good grazing. The Scarthwaite vestry answered that the stream marking the boundary *did* run 300 yards to the east, as anyone could see. It did. The Ulpha Rigg vestry minutes, now held in the Cumbria Archive, record that the stream had "altered its course without flood or fall." In 1784 Ulpha Rigg began sending its own walkers on the Scarthwaite bounds-walk, to recite over the Scarthwaite parishioners. The stream returned to its former course.

The second item is a note written into the vestry book in 1936 by the parish clerk, Mr. Josiah Tyson, on the occasion of the last bounds-walk:

> Mr. Hotine's men have come with their instruments and taken the Knott Stone away and put up their pillar in its place, and we are told that the Ordnance maps will keep the bounds hereafter and there is no more need for walking them. I have said to them that it is a great thing they are taking on and I hope they know it. They were very civil.

## Addendum 041.2: Discovery

In the 1938 publication of the retriangulation results, the height of Harrow Knott was given as 515.1 m. This was an error introduced when a field observation was transcribed at Southampton. The true height was, and had been, 512.1 m. The error was not noticed. The OS 1:10,560 sheet covering Harrow Knott was revised in 1951 and printed the height as 515 m.

In 1953, Ordnance Survey field surveyor Mr. Arthur Pennock re-levelled Harrow Knott as part of routine revision and found its summit at 515.1 m. The value was confirmed and the file closed.

In 1958 the transcription error was found during an audit of the original 1937 field books. The height of Harrow Knott was formally corrected to 512.1 m. In 1959 Pennock re-levelled the summit to check the correction, and found it at 512.1 m.

Pennock was a careful surveyor with twenty-two years' service, and he did not believe he had measured the same hill twice to within a centimetre and been three metres wrong the first time. He spent his own leave in 1960 and 1961 working through the revision history of the dale. He found forty-one cases in which the ground conformed to a recorded value later shown to be a transcription or computation error, and then conformed again when the error was corrected. He took his findings to his superiors. When he was told that he had found forty-one of his own mistakes, he conducted an experiment.

The following letter was written by Pennock to the Director General of the Ordnance Survey. It was intercepted by a Foundation asset at Southampton, and is the reason SCPG-041 came to the Foundation's attention.

> Sir,
>
> I have submitted with my revision returns for sheet NY 21 NW an entry for a sheepfold at grid reference ████ ████, on the east shoulder of Harrow Knott. There was no sheepfold at that place. I know the ground there as well as I know my own garden, and there was nothing there but heather.
>
> The return was checked and approved on the 3rd of this month. On the 9th I went up to see. There is now a sheepfold there, of dry-stone construction, roughly circular, about eight yards across, and of exactly the dimensions I gave. It looks two hundred years old. There is lichen on it.
>
> I do not know what I have done and I do not expect you to believe me. I ask you to send any surveyor you trust to the place, and then to consider very carefully, before anyone at Southampton alters anything on the sheets for this dale again, what it is we are drawing.
>
> I have the honour to be, Sir, your obedient servant,
>
> A. Pennock

The sheepfold remains at the recorded location and is designated SCPG-041-1. It is the first feature known to have been *created* by an entry in the authoritative record, as distinct from moved or altered by one. Pennock was recruited to the Foundation in 1962 and led field survey at Site-114 until his retirement in 1979. Outside the test series in Addendum 041.4, he never again submitted a record he had not measured, and he required the same of his staff. Protocol FLUSH BRACKET is largely his work.

## Addendum 041.3: Incident 041-09 (Low Gill Cottage)

On 17 November 2009, an automated data-cleansing routine was run across the OS MasterMap Topography Layer to remove duplicate building polygons. These were an artefact of a 2001 data migration, in which some buildings had been recorded twice at the same position. The routine ran as a batch process outside the edit workflow and so was never routed to the Liaison Desk. It deleted 11,840 duplicate polygons nationally. Within the Zone it deleted one.

That polygon was not a duplicate. Low Gill Cottage, a two-storey stone house built in 1823, had been extended in 1998. The extension was recorded as a separate polygon adjoining the original footprint, and for a period in 2001 the two polygons had been assigned the same identifier. The cleansing routine treated them as duplicates and kept the extension. It deleted the original house.

The deletion was committed at 23:40. At the time, Low Gill Cottage was occupied by its owner, Mrs. Ellen Ashby, 79, a widow who had lived in the house since 1956.

At 07:15 on 18 November, a Royal Mail carrier found the original house gone. The 1998 extension stood alone, a single-storey kitchen and bathroom open along one wall to the weather where it had joined the house. The ground where the house had stood was turf, level and continuous with the garden and apparently undisturbed. Mrs. Ashby's car was parked in its usual place beside it. Her garden wall, recorded as a separate feature, was intact, and so was her washing line. Mrs. Ashby was not found and has not been found. No remains, possessions, or traces of the house's contents were recovered by ground-penetrating radar or excavation to a depth of 4 m.

A Site-114 response team was on scene by 09:00. At 11:20 the Liaison Desk restored the deleted polygon from the previous version of the record, in the hope that restoring the house would also restore anything that had been inside it. The restoration was confirmed on the ground at first light on 20 November.

What stands on the site is what the record describes, and the record describes only the outline of a building, its height, and its roof type. The structure now on the original footprint of Low Gill Cottage is a solid block of local slate-stone, 9.1 by 6.4 m in plan, 7.2 m to the ridge of a pitched roof, and consistent in material and weathering with a building of 1823. It has no doors or windows, no chimneys, and no interior. Ground-penetrating radar and a 2010 core sample show it to be solid stone throughout. It is designated SCPG-041-2.

No trace of Mrs. Ashby was found in the core sample. The Foundation does not know whether any would have been. Nobody at the Foundation knows what happens to an occupant when a building is removed from the record, or whether restoring the record could ever have returned her. Recreating the conditions to find out is prohibited.

The cover story released was a structural collapse caused by subsidence into old mine workings, followed by rebuilding of the site as a memorial cairn by the county. Mrs. Ashby's son, Mr. David Ashby, was told that his mother died in the collapse. He was not told that the stone structure standing where the house stood is, by the only definition SCPG-041 recognizes, his mother's house.

The bulk-process exclusion described in Protocol FLUSH BRACKET, item 4, was inserted into OS production systems two weeks later.

## Addendum 041.4: Recorded Features Test Series, 1963–1971

The following tests were conducted under Pennock's supervision before the Ethics Committee halted further creation testing in 1971. Each feature was entered into the authoritative record through the Liaison Desk at an unoccupied location within the Zone, and was deleted after inspection, except where noted.

| Feature recorded | Result on the ground |
|---|---|
| Cairn, 2 m | Conical pile of local stone, 2.00 m. Stones are fitted. The cairn has no hollow. |
| Spring | Water issues from level ground at the recorded point. Flow is steady and does not vary with rainfall. Water is potable and was found to be chemically identical to rainwater that fell on Harrow Knott in 1936. |
| Footbridge over Scar Beck | Flat slab of stone spanning the beck at the recorded width. No railings (not recorded). Bears load. |
| Building, 1 storey, 4 × 4 m, flat roof | Solid block of local stone, as in Incident 041-09. |
| Building, 1 storey, 4 × 4 m, flat roof, with "entrance" point feature recorded on its southern edge | Solid block, with a rectangular recess 0.9 m deep on its southern face. The recess does not continue into the interior because there is no interior. |
| Woodland, coniferous, 0.2 ha | Stand of 94 trees, all larch, all the same height, in rows. The OS woodland symbol was historically printed in rows. |
| Area of rough grassland | No change. Already present. |
| Triangulation pillar (second pillar, 40 m from S5410) | Standard OS pillar, correct in every detail, including a flush bracket bearing a number that does not appear in any OS register. Not deleted: the 1964 minutes record that "nobody on the survey team was willing to be the one to remove it." |

Pennock's summary of the series:

> The land does what the map says, and the map says very little. It says where things are and how big they are and what they are called. It does not say what they are *for*. A house on the map is an outline and a height. On the map you cannot go into a house.
>
> Put plainly: in the Zone we can make anything we can draw, and nothing we cannot, and the things a surveyor draws are not the things a person lives in.

## Addendum 041.5: The Question of Authority

*Note appended to the 2026 review by Ms. Priya Venkataraman, Senior Cartographer, Foundation Liaison Desk, Southampton.*

For eighty years, protecting SCPG-041 has meant protecting a single database. We have done it well. The Desk reviewed fourteen proposed edits to the Zone last year and all of them were surveyed, verified, and committed. None of them changed anything, because by the time the ground had been measured, the ground already agreed.

In the spring of 2024 a new footpath appeared across the northern flank of Harrow Knott. It runs from the Scarthwaite road to the summit by a line no path has ever taken, across a stretch of bog the older footpaths go around. The bog is gone from under the new path, which is firm, well-drained, and about a metre wide, and has been worn in by walkers. It is not in the Ordnance Survey record. It first appeared in a commercial navigation platform used by roughly two thirds of the smartphones in the United Kingdom. Its mapping of footpaths is generated automatically from aggregated GPS traces and satellite imagery. The path was generated in error from the traces of a group of fell runners who crossed the bog in the dry summer of 2022.

I want to be precise about what this means. SCPG-041 has not changed. It still conforms the ground to the authoritative record, as it did for the Scarthwaite vestry and then for the Ordnance Survey. It has never cared who keeps the record. The record is whatever people defer to when they want to know what the ground is like. For most of the twentieth century that was the Ordnance Survey. For most of the country now it is the map on their phone, and a walker who finds the phone disagreeing with the ground will generally assume the phone is right.

Within the Zone, that walker is correct.

The platform in question made about 31,000 automated edits to features within the Zone in the past twelve months. They were made by a model trained on imagery, and no human reviewed them. The Foundation has no desk inside that company. Negotiations to establish one are in progress and are not going well, partly because the company does not have a meaningful concept of an authoritative record of any single place, only a continuously updated estimate of all of them. Its engineers call this ground truth. They mean the imagery they train the model on. I did not have the heart to tell them.

Most of the 31,000 edits changed nothing. The ground already agreed, or the edits were too small to see. We have catalogued 187 that changed something. Most of those are sub-metre shifts in the lines of walls. A barn at High Shaw Farm has rotated four degrees. A tarn on the western shoulder has become more regular in outline, because the model rounds the corners of water bodies. The larch plantation from the 1963 tests, which we could not bring ourselves to delete, has been reclassified by the platform as mixed woodland. Birch are coming up between the rows.

We check the addressing layer every morning. The platform has not yet deleted a house.

Interim guidance for Site-114 personnel, effective immediately:

- Navigation applications are not to be used within the Zone for any purpose. This is not because they are inaccurate.
- Personnel who become aware of a disagreement between a navigation application and the terrain within the Zone are to report it to the Desk immediately. Do not report it to the application's feedback service. That service is authoritative too.
- Personnel are reminded that the stone structure at Low Gill is not a memorial. It is a house.
